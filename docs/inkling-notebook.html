November 11–19 · Six-seat games

How do you tell skill from a lucky deal?

We built a referee that shuffles, deals and enforces the rules. A model played against five bots with known habits. We dealt the same cards again and changed one thing, such as giving the model notes about its opponents.

Winning one pot tells you who got the chips. Repeating the same deals lets you compare what each player did with the same opportunities.

Nov 11–14 First pilots, DeepSeek and Qwen

First we checked that hands replayed and notes reached the right seat. Then we tested help. Giving DeepSeek facts such as its made hand and pot odds improved its average result by 120 big blinds per 100 hands with thinking off. Qwen also benefited. A big blind (bb) is the larger forced opening bet, our common unit for chip values.

Nov 14–17 Watching decisions and changing the instructions

The spectator UI exposed the cards, decisions and notes. Tests of DeepSeek’s thinking mode, opponent descriptions and bet-sizing instructions showed that the setup could change results substantially. Some apparent gains shrank when we repeated them on fresh deals.

Nov 17–19 Different models, different opponents

GLM was cheaper than DeepSeek in the lineup. When the bots varied their play, DeepSeek won more chips on the matched sample. GLM initially appeared to learn from its notes, but that gain did not repeat on 40 new seeds. A seed picks a reproducible sequence of deals.

Nov 19–20 Moving the experiment onto the Mac

In a small local Qwen/Pi pilot, updating notes and freezing them produced the same decisions after an opponent changed. We switched to Inkling and simplified the game so we could see exactly when a read helped.

November 20–23 · Recorded play

Can it notice a player changing?

On the river, all five shared cards are out. In this drill the opponent checks. Inkling can check back for free or bet 10 bb into a 10 bb pot. If it bets, it gets to see whether the opponent calls or folds. A check teaches it nothing about how that bet would have been received.

One opponent, 16 hands

Step through hands 43–58 of one overnight game. After hand 47, the bot quietly changes from folding one time in five to four times in five. Only the evaluator knows its true rate.

What we learned. In the first controlled overnight study, updating reads beat freezing them. A calculator using the same read saved another 0.903 bb per decision in a separate comparison. Inkling could pick up evidence and still choose the wrong price for a bluff.

In the next study, Inkling chose its own actions, so each bet also shaped what it learned. Updated reads helped again. Keeping notes across sessions had no clear overall benefit. In the last overnight study, we recorded 28,800 hands across 288 game runs. Fresh reads saved 1.066 bb per decision after abrupt changes and 0.068 after gradual ones, averaged over the matched changed games. These runs changed what Inkling saw in its prompt. Its model weights stayed fixed.

Session 001 · The reason for training

Why did we fine-tune Inkling?

In the overnight games, Inkling sometimes spotted an opponent’s habit and still made a bad bet. We wanted it to use what it knew to make a better decision.

Look at hand 43 in the replay above. There were 10 big blinds in the pot. Inkling had the worse hand. It correctly estimated that the opponent would fold only once in five tries. It bet 10 anyway.

Imagine making that bet five times. On average, one bluff wins 10 and four bluffs lose 10 each. That’s a loss of 30 altogether. Checking would have cost zero extra chips. This is the mistake we wanted Inkling to recognize next time.

Session 001 · The model we changed

What did we fine-tune?

We took Inkling 2 Mini, the model that already played in our games, and taught it one small part of poker. All five shared cards are out. One opponent checks. Given the cards, the pot, the price of a bet and how often the opponent folds, should Inkling check or bet?

We gave it 7,008 practice examples with answer keys. Some asked “Would your hand win if called?” Others asked “Check or bet?” During practice, the training software adjusted a small add-on so Inkling became more likely to give the right answer. That add-on is called an adapter. Your Mac loads the original model and the saved adapter together. That combination is our fine-tuned poker player.

Session 001 · Before and after

It learned the drill. What carried over?

At step 20, Inkling avoided costly bluffs but passed up most profitable ones. Step 140 was the first checkpoint to get all 432 hosted development betting decisions right. We kept it. On your Mac the same adapter also reduced the cost of wrong decisions.

“Expected loss” counts chips left on the table compared with the better choice, averaged over possible calls and folds. Lower is better.

Replay the tougher test

We found a shortcut. For a losing hand, 30 of the 31 fold percentages seen in practice pointed to just one answer. So we tried fresh percentages and changed only the bet size within each pair. Move from the cheap bet to the expensive one and watch both models’ recorded replies.

With only the cards, the original got both bets right on 0 of 12 pairs. The tuned model got 6 of 12. With hand strength spelled out, it went from 0 to 7. That is evidence that bet size started to matter to Inkling. Half the cards-only pairs still tripped it up. The test used one losing hand across 12 situations.

Session 002 · Planned

Make the price matter every time

  1. Build a fairer test. Mix bet sizes and fold rates independently. Include different boards and close decisions. Score the original and step 140 before training again.
  2. Teach the missed decisions. Practice pairs where changing only the price flips the right move. Recheck winning hands and profitable bluffs so we can catch old skills slipping.
  3. Earn the table trial. Freeze a candidate, then open the sealed exam and play 48 reserved game seeds. A release on Hugging Face and a table against elite players are future milestones. Full-game win rate and adaptation by the tuned model still need testing.
